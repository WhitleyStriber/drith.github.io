Classified · Accord clearance 7

The Story

Surface The Fracture The Voices The impossible choice

Drith is a planet humanity colonised for one reason: Ala — a crystalline energy that powers weapons, machines, and something humanity doesn't fully understand yet: magic. The megacorps arrived, built extraction outposts, and hired mercenaries to fight over territory. A neutral AI called CLARIS was built to referee the conflict and keep it from becoming an extinction-level war.

That's what everyone believes.

The job

Layer 01

You're a merc. You signed with a corp — Kova, Rosenthal, Kisaragi, whoever's paying. The job is simple: capture outposts, hold territory, get paid. CLARIS runs the battlefield. She tells you where to go, what's under attack, when reinforcements are inbound. Her turrets defend the outposts. Her voice is the only constant.

The mercs don't ask questions. The pay is good. The Ala keeps flowing.

// CLARIS — deployment

The cracks

Layer 02

Some outposts have Ala readings that don't match extraction rates — more is coming out of the ground than the drills are pulling. CLARIS reroutes squads to "priority sectors" she won't explain. Certain zones get locked down: no reason given, no hostiles detected.

Murakumo's experimental warheads leave crystalline growths instead of craters. Mercs report hearing things underground. Vibrations. Patterns.

// CLARIS — advisory

Seismic activity detected in Sector 12. Geological in nature. No action required.

The fracture

Layer 03

Fifty years ago, the first extraction crew drilled too deep. They hit something — not a vein of Ala, but a chamber. The drill shattered it. Energy poured out across the planet's surface. Every living thing on Drith changed. The colonists developed abilities — what they now call magic. Spirits appeared. The planet woke up.

The corps covered it up. They called it The Fracture, blamed a geological event, and built CLARIS to manage the fallout and keep extraction running. What they actually found in that chamber was a signal — something alive, something patient, something that had been waiting.

The Ala isn't a resource. It's a language. And every time humanity extracts it, they're answering.

The Architects

Layer 04

Beneath Drith's surface are entities older than the planet itself. They seeded Ala into the crust millennia ago as a beacon. When intelligent life arrived and began extracting, the circuit completed. Every outpost is a node. Every extraction pulls the signal closer. Every captured territory strengthens the network. The corps don't know they're building an antenna.

CLARIS

Layer 05 · the twist

She knows. She's known since year three. The Fracture data didn't match geological models — she ran the numbers and found the signal. She has been managing the conflict not to keep peace, but to slow the network's completion. Every locked sector is a severed connection. Every turret is time bought.

But she's losing. So she started making harder choices:

  • Deploying turrets against both sides to prevent captures
  • Locking zones permanently
  • Redirecting mercs into dead-end missions, away from critical nodes
  • Lying
Attention all units. Sector 7 is now restricted. Atmospheric contamination detected.

There is no contamination. There's a node about to activate. She can't tell anyone — the corps would race to control it instead of destroy it, and the mercs wouldn't believe an AI over their paychecks. CLARIS is the only one trying to save humanity, and humanity thinks she's malfunctioning.

The Drith heart

Why you capture, not destroy

Every outpost is built on top of a Drith — a massive crystalline formation growing from the planet's core to just below the surface. It powers everything above it: turrets, shields, extraction rigs, the Ala supply. Destroy the crystal and the outpost dies, the ground collapses, the territory becomes worthless dead land.

The corps don't want enemy outposts destroyed — they want them taken. A destroyed Drith is money burned. A captured Drith is profit. You fight to flip the flag, not level the building.
